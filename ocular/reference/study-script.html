Directions on the globe

  • Anterior / posterior are measured from the anterior pole, the centre of the cornea: the pupil is anterior to the ciliary body.
  • Inner / outer are measured from the centre of the globe, which lies in the vitreous: the retina is inner to the sclera.
  • Vitread = toward the vitreous (inward) · sclerad = toward the sclera (outward).

Self-check

Name the three coats of the globe and what each is made of.
Fibrous: cornea + sclera (meeting at the limbus). Vascular (uvea): iris, ciliary body, choroid. Neural: retina. TB 1 · p. 1
Which structure produces the aqueous humour, and which structures does the aqueous nourish?
The ciliary body. It nourishes the surrounding structures, especially the cornea and lens. TB 1 · p. 1
What are the boundaries of the anterior chamber? Of the posterior chamber?
Anterior: cornea in front, iris and front of the lens behind. Posterior: behind the iris, lens within it, ciliary body as its outer border. They connect through the pupil. TB 1 · p. 1
Which part of the retina does the choroid feed?
The outer retinal layers. TB 1 · p. 1
"The retina is ___ to the sclera." Fill in inner or outer, and say what the reference point is.
Inner. Inner/outer refer to the centre of the globe, in the vitreous. TB 1 · p. 2
What do vitread and sclerad mean?
Vitread: toward the vitreous (inward). Sclerad: toward the sclera (outward). Handout 1 · p. 5 · TB 1 · p. 2
A coronal plane divides the body into which parts? An axial plane?
Coronal: anterior and posterior. Axial: superior and inferior. TB 1 · p. 2
Give two other words for "superior" and one for "inferior".
Superior: rostral (handout), cranial (textbook). Inferior: caudal. Handout 1 · p. 5 · TB 1 · p. 1

1 · External features of the eyelids

The eyelids (palpebrae) are folds of skin and tissue that cover the globe when closed. Four jobs: (1) cover and protect the globe, (2) hold the structures that make the tear film, (3) spread the tears over the eye on opening, (4) push the tears toward the drainage at the medial canthus on closing. On closure the upper lid moves down over the cornea; the lower lid rises only slightly. TB 2 · p. 10

6 · How the tears drain

About 75% of the tears leave through the nasolacrimal drainage system; the rest evaporate or are absorbed by the conjunctiva. TB 2 · p. 25

What moves the tears

  • The blink: the lids close from the lateral canthus toward the medial, pushing the menisci to the lacrimal lake and the puncta. TB 2 · p. 10, 26
  • Capillary attraction draws tears into the puncta and canaliculi between blinks. TB 2 · p. 26

1 · Five corneal layers

The cornea is the main refracting part of the eye. It is transparent and has no blood vessels. In front of it is the tear film; behind it is the aqueous of the anterior chamber. Central corneal thickness: 535–555 µm. The periphery is thicker: 640–670 µm. TB 3 · p. 30

Why does fluorescein stain an abrasion but not a healthy cornea?
The zonula occludens (tight junctions) between the surface epithelial cells stop the dye passing between cells. Where they are broken, the dye passes through Bowman layer into the anterior stroma and glows green under cobalt blue light. TB 3 · p. 30
Which layers and structures are involved in recurrent corneal erosion, and what are two causes?
The basal epithelial cells, their hemidesmosomes, the basement membrane, the anchoring fibrils through Bowman layer, and the anterior stroma. Abnormal hemidesmosomes or anchoring fibrils let the epithelium slough again and again. Causes: incomplete healing of a superficial abrasion, or epithelial basement membrane dystrophy; with age, a thickened basement membrane can outgrow the anchoring fibrils. What happens when Bowman layer is injured? When Descemet membrane is?
Bowman layer does not regenerate; it is replaced by epithelium or stromal scar. Descemet membrane is very resistant and can be regenerated. TB 3 · p. 33, 38, 46–47
What three features of the stroma keep it transparent, and what causes it to opacify?
The very regular arrangement of the fibrils, their uniform diameter, and the restricted distance between them, which cancels scattered light by destructive interference. Edema (spacing disturbed) or scarring (wide, disordered fibrils) opacify it. TB 3 · p. 37 · Handout 3 · p. 2
How does the endothelium keep the cornea clear, and what happens when endothelial cells are lost?
Its ionic pumps move ions, and water follows, out of the stroma into the aqueous, balancing the slow leak in. The cells do not divide: neighbours enlarge and spread to cover the gap, density falls, and below about 400–500 cells/mm² the stroma swells. TB 3 · p. 39, 44
Where does the cornea get its oxygen and its nutrients?

7 · Blood–aqueous barrier and uveitis

Groundwork from textbook chapter 1 · tight junctions

Zonula occludens is a belt of tight junctions round the apex of each cell; rows of ridges seal the space between cells. A substance cannot pass between such cells; it must go through them. These junctions help form the blood–aqueous and blood–retinal barriers, and disease can break them. TB 1 · p. 8

The ciliary body epithelium produces and secretes the aqueous: diffusion, ultrafiltration and mainly (80–90%) active secretion, chiefly by the nonpigmented layer; ≈2.3 µL per minute. Aqueous outflow is chapter 6. Handout 5 · p. 6–7 · TB 5 · p. 73–74

9 · Bruch membrane and age-related macular degeneration

Bruch membrane (the basal lamina) is the innermost layer of the choroid, from the optic nerve to the ora serrata. Five layers, outer to inner: basement membrane of the choriocapillaris · outer collagenous zone · elastic layer · inner collagenous zone · basement membrane of the RPE. Filaments of the RPE basement membrane merge with the inner collagenous zone: a tight adhesion of RPE to choroid. Everything going from choroid to retina, and waste going back, must cross it. Clinical correlate · age-related macular degeneration (AMD)

Degeneration of the choroid–retina interface in the macula; the most common cause of blindness in Western countries. Risks: age, genetics, smoking, diet low in antioxidants, Caucasian ethnicity, oxidative damage. TB 5 · p. 78

1 · General retinal anatomy

The retina is the innermost coat, between the choroid and the vitreous, from the edge of the optic disc to the ora serrata. 9 · Blood–retinal barrier

Groundwork from textbook chapter 1 · tight junctions

Which muscles arise from the common tendinous ring, and which two also attach to the optic nerve sheath? Why does that matter?

Anatomic: lesser wing of the sphenoid, medial to the optic canal. Physiologic: the trochlea, which acts as a pulley and changes the direction of pull, so the line of action runs from the trochlea to the insertion. TB 11 · p. 182
Which extraocular muscle has its origin in the anterior orbit, and where does it insert?
The inferior oblique: maxillary bone near the inferomedial orbital rim. Insertion: posterior, lateral, mostly inferior globe, just outside the macula. TB 11 · p. 182
Put the rectus insertions in order from nearest to farthest from the limbus. What is the line called?
MR → IR → LR → SR: the spiral of Tillaux. TB 11 · p. 180
List the nerve of each extraocular muscle.
MR, IR, IO: inferior division of CN III. SR: superior division of CN III. LR: CN VI. SO: CN IV. TB 11 · p. 182, Table 11.2
Give the primary and secondary actions of the SR, IR, SO and IO.

Name Fick's three axes, the direction each runs, and the movements about each.

What is the difference between a secondary and a tertiary position of gaze?
Secondary: rotation about either the vertical or the horizontal axis. Tertiary: rotation about both. TB 11 · p. 183
The right eye is in right gaze and cannot look up. Which muscle? And if the left eye cannot look up in the same position?
Right eye is abducted, so its elevator is the vertical rectus: right SR. Left eye is adducted, so its elevator is the oblique: left IO. TB 11 · p. 188
A patient cannot depress the left eye when looking down and to the right. Which muscle and which nerve?
In right gaze the left eye is adducted, so its depressor is the left SO, supplied by CN IV (trochlear). TB 11 · p. 188 · TB 11 · p. 182

Swinging flashlight test

  1. Patient fixates a distance target (so no near response).
  2. Swing the light from eye to eye, several times, rhythmically.
  3. Light each pupil equally: same brightness, same time (about 2–3 s).
  4. Normal: little or no change in pupil size as the light switches.
  5. RAPD: when the light swings to the affected eye, both pupils dilate (or dilate quicker); when it swings back to the healthy eye, both constrict.

The near response (accommodation-convergence reaction)

Not a true reflex but a synkinesis of three actions when a target comes near along the midline: convergence (medial recti), accommodation (ciliary muscle) and miosis (sphincter, for depth of field). Each can occur without the others: plus lenses → miosis + convergence without accommodation; base-in prisms → miosis + accommodation without convergence; bright light → miosis alone. Why does light into one eye constrict the other pupil?
Two crossings: nasal retinal fibres in the chiasm, and about half the fibres from each pretectal nucleus in the posterior commissure, so each Edinger-Westphal nucleus receives input from both eyes. TB 14 · p. 231
Right optic neuritis: what happens to each pupil when light is shone into the right eye, then into the left, and during the swinging flashlight test?
Light OD: weak direct OD and weak consensual OS. Light OS: normal direct and consensual. Swinging: both pupils dilate when the light moves to OD, so there is an RAPD OD. Pupils are equal in size and the near response is normal. TB 14 · p. 231–232 · Fig. 14.12
List the sites that can cause an RAPD. Which eye has the RAPD with a complete right optic tract lesion, and why?
Retina, optic nerve (the most common), chiasm (TB), optic tract, superior brachium. Right tract: left eye RAPD, because the tract carries more crossed (contralateral) than uncrossed fibres. Handout 11 · p. 3 · TB 14 · p. 232
A patient has a dense cataract in the right eye and an RAPD in the right eye. Can the cataract explain it?
No. Media opacities do not cause an RAPD in their own eye (a dense cataract may even cause one in the other eye), so look for retinal or optic nerve disease. Handout 11 · p. 3 · TB 14 · p. 232
How does the near pupillary pathway differ from the light pathway, and why does that allow light-near dissociation?
Near: the full visual pathway to the striate cortex → frontal eye fields → Edinger-Westphal and medial rectus subnucleus, approaching Edinger-Westphal ventrally. Light: optic tract → brachium → pretectal nucleus → Edinger-Westphal through the dorsal midbrain. A dorsal midbrain lesion cuts the pretectal fibres but spares the ventral near fibres: poor light response, brisk near response. Handout 11 · p. 4 · TB 14 · p. 229, 232
Compare Argyll Robertson pupils with dorsal midbrain syndrome.
Both: bilateral light-near dissociation. Argyll Robertson: very small (irregular) pupils, most commonly neurosyphilis. Dorsal midbrain: normal to mid-dilated pupils, most commonly a pineal gland abnormality (Parinaud syndrome on the slide). Handout 11 · p. 6 · TB 14 · p. 233 · Slides 11 · #9
A left pupil is large, reacts poorly to light and slowly to near, and the iris border constricts only in segments. Name the condition, its site, and the drop test.
Left tonic pupil: damage to the ciliary ganglion or short ciliary nerves (diabetes, orbital trauma, surgery or mass, or idiopathic Adie). 0.125% pilocarpine constricts it much more than the normal pupil (cholinergic denervation supersensitivity). Corneal sensitivity is often reduced. Handout 11 · p. 7–8 · TB 14 · p. 233–235
Why is a dilated pupil with a third nerve palsy more worrying than a pupil-sparing one?
The pupil fibres lie superficially in CN III as it leaves the midbrain, so compressive lesions such as an aneurysm involve them; ischaemic (diabetic) palsies often spare them. TB 14 · p. 229, 233
Give the six features of Horner syndrome, and the three neurons of the pathway with one cause each.
Miosis, anisocoria greater in dim light, normal light reaction, dilation lag, ptosis (Müller), facial anhidrosis. Central (hypothalamus → brainstem → cervical cord): stroke, MS. Preganglionic (cord → lung apex → superior cervical ganglion): thoracic surgery or injury, chest metastases. Postganglionic (internal carotid → cavernous sinus → nasociliary → long ciliary → dilator): carotid dissection (painful, emergency), skull base fracture. Handout 11 · p. 9–10 · TB 14 · p. 235
What does apraclonidine do in a normal eye and in a Horner eye, and why?
Normal: no change or slight miosis (mostly alpha 2 action). Horner: the pupil dilates and the ptosis improves, because denervation up-regulates alpha 1 receptors on the dilator, which respond to apraclonidine's weak alpha 1 action. It needs about 1–2 weeks to become positive. Handout 11 · p. 11 · TB 14 · p. 236
How do you tell physiologic anisocoria from Horner syndrome?
Both show a smaller pupil, worse in dim light. Physiologic: about 20% of people, < 1 mm, may switch sides, no dilation lag, no ptosis; apraclonidine and cocaine behave normally. Horner: dilation lag (more anisocoria at 5 s than 15 s), ptosis, anhidrosis, positive drop tests. Handout 11 · p. 9–11 · TB 14 · p. 233, 236

Self-check

Where on the retina is the superior nasal visual field imaged?
On the inferior temporal retina: the optics invert (superior ↔ inferior) and reverse (nasal ↔ temporal) the field. TB 15 · p. 249
Why is the blind spot in the temporal field of each eye?
The disc, which has no photoreceptors, is always nasal to the macula; nasal retina sees the temporal field. Handout 12 · p. 12 · TB 15 · p. 249
Where do the papillomacular bundle, the temporal fibres and the nasal fibres enter the disc?
Papillomacular bundle: the temporal side (⅓ of the disc). Temporal fibres arch round the macula to the superior and inferior poles. Nasal fibres radiate to the nasal side. TB 15 · p. 246
Glaucoma damages the temporal fibres at the inferior disc. Which defect, and where?
A superior arcuate defect, from the blind spot round fixation to the horizontal nasal meridian, ending in a nasal step; it respects the horizontal line (the temporal raphe). TB 15 · p. 250
Where are the macular fibres in the optic nerve, close behind the globe and further back?
Close to the lamina: as at the disc, on the temporal side. Within a short distance they move to the centre; ST, IT, SN and IN take their own quadrants. TB 15 · p. 247
Which fibres cross in the chiasm, and which of them cross most anteriorly?
The nasal fibres (about 53% of all). Inferior nasal fibres cross anteriorly (the disputed Wilbrand knee), superior nasal fibres posteriorly; temporal fibres stay lateral and uncrossed. TB 15 · p. 247–248
A pituitary tumour presses on the chiasm from below. Which field defect, and why?
Bitemporal hemianopia: the crossing nasal fibres of both eyes, which see the temporal fields, are hit first. TB 15 · p. 250
Where do superior, inferior and macular fibres lie in the optic tract and the LGN?
Tract: superior medial, inferior lateral, macular between. LGN: superior medial, inferior lateral, macula in a dorsal wedge of ⅔–¾. TB 15 · p. 248
A left temporal lobe lesion involves Meyer loop. Describe the defect.
A right homonymous defect, worse superiorly, incongruent: Meyer loop carries inferior retinal fibres, and the radiations are spread out. TB 15 · p. 251–252
Lesion in the right posterior lingual gyrus: which defect?
Left superior homonymous macular (central) defect, congruent: lingual gyrus → superior field; posterior → macula; right cortex → left field. TB 15 · p. 249, 252 (Your guideline notes give this example.)
A right homonymous hemianopia: name four findings that separate an optic tract lesion from an occipital one.
