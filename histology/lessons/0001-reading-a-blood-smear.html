Reading a blood smear

The seven formed elements, how each one looks under Wright stain, and the numbers the script says to memorise.

~15 minSource: Script, Ch. 1 (Blood Cells and Hematopoiesis)Next: hematopoiesis & growth factors
The one win for today

Given a one-line description of a cell on a smear — nucleus shape, granule colour, size, percentage — name the cell. Nearly every MCQ from this chapter is that task in disguise.

1. The map

Blood is a specialised connective tissue: a fluid matrix plus a cellular fraction called the formed elements. There are three groups, and the white cells split once more by whether visible granules are present.

  • Formed elements
    • Red blood cells (erythrocytes)
    • Platelets (thrombocytes)
    • White blood cells (leukocytes)
      • Granulocytes — neutrophil · eosinophil · basophil
      • Agranulocytes — monocyte · lymphocyte (small and large)

2. The cells without a nucleus

Erythrocyte

Anucleate, non-granulated, eosinophilic (takes up the acidic dye eosin → pink). A biconcave disc, 7.2 microns across; the thin centre looks pale. So uniform in size that it is your internal size reference on any smear. Contains hemoglobin, transports oxygen, lives ~120 days. Under the electron microscope the cytoplasm is homogeneous — nothing inside but hemoglobin.

Eye link — the extrude-your-nucleus club

Three cell types extrude the nucleus at maturity and fill up with one class of protein:

  • Red blood cells → hemoglobin
  • Skin keratinocytes → keratins
  • Lens fibres → crystallins

Reticulocyte

An immature red cell released from the marrow early; matures in the blood in 1–2 days. Normal: about 1 per 100 RBCs. Recognised by blue strands and dots inside — leftover RNA-containing organelles (free ribosomes) bind the basic blue dye.

Platelet

Not a cell but a fragment pinched off a megakaryocyte in the marrow — membrane, cytoplasm and granules, never a nucleus. On a smear: small purple specks between the much larger red cells. Job: hemostasis.

Reference values the script says to memorise
  • Size 2–3 microns
  • Concentration 150,000 – 450,000 per ml (the script's wording)
  • Lifetime 5–11 days

Inside: a marginal microtubule band around the rim (keeps the resting disc shape, contracts on activation), mitochondria, an open canalicular system of membrane channels, and three granule types:

GranuleContents
α (alpha)proteoglycans, hemostasis factors and cofactors
δ (delta) = dense bodiesserotonin and Ca++
λ (lambda)lysosomes

3. Making the colours: the two stains

Both are a mix of an acidic and a basic dye, so acidophilic cytoplasm and basophilic nucleus come out in contrasting colours.

StainWhenComponents
Wrightroutine smearsEosin → cytoplasm pink · Methylene blue → nuclear material & nucleic acids blue
Giemsaif a systemic microbial disease is suspectedEosin · Methylene blue · Azure B (especially good at identifying reticulocytes)

Expected colours: erythrocytes pink · platelets light pale pink · lymphocyte cytoplasm sky blue · monocyte cytoplasm pale blue · leukocyte nuclear chromatin magenta.

A good film is smooth, even and tongue-shaped, thinning to a feathered edge; a bad one is streaky with holes and ridges and its cells cannot be evaluated.

Band neutrophils

An intermediate step before the segmented neutrophil: nucleus horseshoe-shaped, not yet segmented. Normal adults: 3–5% of WBCs (up to 0.7 × 10⁹/L).

Clinical note — "left shift"

More bands than that means the marrow has been signalled to release and make more WBCs — most often infection or inflammation. Too many horseshoe nuclei on a smear is a real sign, not a staining artefact.

Two cells you never see in the blood

  • Plasma cell — derived from B lymphocytes; makes antibodies.
  • Histiocyte — a macrophage derived from extravascular monocytes.
Eye link — spotting a plasma cell in tissue

The exam likes to show a micrograph and ask "which cell?". A plasma cell gives itself away three times over: the nucleus sits off-centre (eccentric); its chromatin is in coarse clumps around the rim — the "clock-face" or cartwheel pattern; and next to the nucleus there is a pale, unstained patch in otherwise deep-blue cytoplasm — the Golgi region where antibodies are packaged. Deep-blue cytoplasm = a lot of rough ER = a cell making a lot of protein. Compare with a lymphocyte, whose nucleus is central and nearly fills the cell.

5. The recognition tree

Ask the questions in this order and you land on one answer.

  • Is there a nucleus?
    • No → pink disc ~7 µm with a pale centre = erythrocyte (blue dots/strands inside → reticulocyte) · tiny purple speck 2–3 µm = platelet
    • Yes → are large coloured granules obvious?
      • Yes → pink/red granules, bilobed nucleus = eosinophil · purple-blue granules hiding the nucleus = basophil
      • No → multi-lobed nucleus, faint granules = neutrophil (horseshoe nucleus → band) · biggest cell, kidney-bean nucleus, pale-blue cytoplasm = monocyte · small cell, round dense nucleus, thin rim of sky-blue cytoplasm = lymphocyte
