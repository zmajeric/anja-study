1. The three tunics

From the lumen outwards: tunica intima → tunica media → tunica adventitia. What distinguishes an aorta from a capillary is not which layers exist but whether a layer is present at all and how thick and specialised it is. The components to be able to name on a diagram, inside → out:

  1. Lumen, lined by the endothelium of the intima
  2. Basal lamina of the endothelium, then subendothelial connective tissue
  3. Internal elastic lamina — outer border of the intima
  4. Smooth muscle of the media
  5. External elastic lamina — outer border of the media
  6. Adventitia — with vasa vasorum and nerves

3. Arteries — who takes the surge

Elastic arteries (aorta; proximal carotid, vertebral, brachiocephalic, subclavian) are the vessels that feel the surge of systole: the wall stretches in systole and recoils in diastole, turning pulsatile output into even flow. Muscular arteries (coronary, superficial temporal, radial, celiac, renal, maxillary, mesenteric — anything large enough to be named) carry a pulse but the surge has already been absorbed upstream. Arterioles (≤150 µm) have no pulse at all — flow is continuous — and their thin muscle coat is the main site of resistance, regulating flow into the capillary bed.

Eye link — two arteries, two classes

The posterior ciliary arteries are muscular arteries. The central retinal artery, after its first few millimetres, is an arteriole.

Clinical note — vasa vasorum and dissecting aneurysm

The aortic wall is so thick that the blood in the lumen cannot keep it alive; small vessels leave the aorta and run within its wall — the vasa vasorum, "vessels of the vessels". With age their openings into the lumen can tear. Blood is driven through the intimal tear and forced between the layers, splitting the media apart: a dissecting aneurysm.

Clinical note — retinal capillaries and pericytes

In a PAS-stained trypsin digest of retinal vessels, endothelial cells have elongated nuclei lying along the vessel; pericytes (mural cells) have rounded nuclei that bulge outward from the wall — dark round dots on the vessel. Pericytes have some contractile properties. The structural rule: pericytes sit within splits of the endothelial basement membrane; smooth muscle cells are layered outside it.

5. The venous side

Venules: all three tunics present but rudimentary. The post-capillary venule still has pericytes and is the classic site of leukocyte emigration and fluid leakage in inflammation. Medium veins accompany muscular arteries: larger, often collapsed lumen and a distinctly thinner wall because venous pressure is low. Large veins accompany elastic arteries; their signature is adventitial longitudinal smooth muscle.

Valves exist below the heart, not above. A valve is a paired fold of the tunica intima: flow towards the heart flattens the leaflets against the wall; reflux fills them so they balloon and meet in the middle.

Clinical note — the soleus, "the second heart"

Venous return from the legs depends on skeletal muscle "milking" the veins. Contraction squeezes the vein, the upper valve opens, blood moves up; relaxation lets blood fall, but the lower valve closes and holds the column. Muscle pump plus valves — neither works alone.

6. Lymphatics — and the retinal AV crossing

Lymphatics collect fluid that escaped from blood capillaries. In a section: very wide, extremely thin-walled, empty-looking channels, usually with no red cells. Lymphatic "capillaries" have an intima and minimal media — more like a capillary than a vein; larger channels have contractile media and valves; the endothelium has "button-like" junctions that let fluid in. In the conjunctiva they sit in the substantia propria beside arterioles.

Eye link — AV nicking, in three sentences

At a retinal arteriovenous crossing the two vessels share one tunica adventitia, so they are bound together. With age, arteriolar sclerosis thickens the arteriole. The venule cannot slide out of the way, so it is focally constricted at the crossing — the "AV nicking" you must recognise ophthalmoscopically.
