0. Warm-up — neurons and glia

Clinical opening — myasthenia gravis

Eye findings are the first and commonest presenting sign in ~50% of patients; limited to the eye = ocular MG. Fluctuating ptosis and diplopia that worsen with fatigue. To reason about it you need normal neuromuscular function — hence this chapter.

2. The sarcomere

Thick filament = myosin: heavy chain carries the head; two α-helical rods form the tail; a hinge between; MLC-1 (essential) and MLC-2 (regulatory) light chains near the head–hinge. Thin filament = actin (myosin-binding site) + tropomyosin (blocks the site) + troponin (TnC binds Ca++; TnT pulls tropomyosin; TnI inhibitory, pulled aside). In cross-section each filament set is a hexagonal lattice.

Sliding filament: filaments slide, they do not shorten. Cross-bridge cycle, one ATP per step: (1) high-energy head with ADP + Pi attaches → (2) working stroke, ADP + Pi released → (3) new ATP binds → detaches, low-energy → (4) ATP split → head re-cocked. Like pulling a clothes line: grab, pull, let go, grab further down.

3. Excitation → contraction → relaxation

Action potential: Na+ in (depolarisation) → Na+ channels close, K+ channels open, K+ out (repolarisation) → rest. It travels down T-tubules (at the A–I junction) to two SR terminal cisternae = a triad. Ryanodine receptors release Ca++ → Ca++ binds TnC → TnT moves tropomyosin and TnI → cross-bridge cycles continue while Ca++ is present. Relaxation: SERCA pumps Ca++ back, held loosely by sequestrin; a Na+–Ca2+ exchanger also exports Ca++. Sarcolemma = the membrane; mitochondria between myofibrils.

Pre-load (length–tension): most tension at optimal overlap; over-stretched heads can't reach, unstretched thin filaments collide — stretch before running. After-load (force–velocity): more load → slower cycling → eventually none.

Clinical notes — four things that go wrong
  • Caffeine sensitises ryanodine receptors → "the shakes".
  • Hypokalemia (K+): weakness, myalgia, tremor. Hypocalcemia (Ca++): tetany — neuronal membranes leak Na+ → depolarisation. Hyponatremia = sodium. Keep the three apart.
  • Tetanus ≠ tetany: Clostridium tetani (soil, manure, rusty nail) → tetanospasmin blocks release of inhibitory impulses at the NMJ → persistent spasm, lock-jaw downward; a disease of nerves. ~10% die; spatula test; Tdap booster every 10 years.

4. The eye's muscles

Eye link — extraocular muscles

Two (maybe three) fibre types, both with sarcomeres, both cholinergic: fast-twitch (well-defined myofibrils; thick, heavily myelinated nerve, one NMJ) and slow/tonic (poorly defined myofibrils; thin nerve, grape-like clusters of NMJs). Smallest motor units of any skeletal muscle → precision and coupled relaxation of the yoked antagonist. Rectus muscles have a global layer (attached to the eye, ~62% fast) and an orbital layer (~81% fast).

Smooth muscle: unitary (GI, bladder, uterus, ureter, iris sphincter; gap junctions) vs multiunit (ciliary muscle, vas deferens; individual motor units, dense autonomic innervation, no gap junctions); vascular = both. Less strength than skeletal but sustains contraction — Mueller's muscle holds the lid open between blinks after the levator lifts it. Contraction: Ca++ mostly from outside via voltage-gated channels (SR modest; also ligand-gated, IP3) → calmodulin → myosin light-chain kinase → myosin phosphorylated → cycling.

Myoepithelium: true epithelial cells (keratins) with basal smooth-muscle-like processes expressing α-smooth-muscle actin, under sympathetic control — gland ducts and the iris dilator (myo basal, pigmented epithelial part apical). Myofibroblasts: sub-epithelial; fibrosis; vimentin, α-SMA (ACTA2), palladin; wound contraction; pericytes are myofibroblast-like; fibroblast → proto-myofibroblast (tension) → myofibroblast (TGF-β1, ED-A fibronectin).

Clinical note — why diabetics dilate poorly

Myoepithelial cells in diabetes accumulate glycogen (lacy vacuolization) that cripples the "myo" component. The dilator is myoepithelium → poor dilation. The sphincter is true smooth muscle → constricts fine.
