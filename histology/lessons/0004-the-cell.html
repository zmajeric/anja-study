Clinical note — why antibiotics work

Chemicals can destroy the prokaryotic cell wall without harming the eukaryotic cell membrane. That difference is the basis of several antibiotic classes.

A virus is not a cell. It is a non-living infectious agent that replicates only inside a living host cell. DNA or RNA in a protein capsid, sometimes with an envelope; surface proteins for attachment; it hijacks the host's protein machinery and the progeny escape by shedding. RNA viruses (HIV) carry reverse transcriptase to make DNA first. Viruses that infect only bacteria are bacteriophages.

2. Membrane and nucleus

Cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer — hydrophilic (polar) heads face in and out, hydrophobic (nonpolar) tails face each other in the middle; by EM it looks like railroad tracks. Floating in it: proteins (receptors, ports, channels, pumps, adhesion), cholesterol, and glycolipids/glycoproteins on the outer surface.

Nucleus — four features to know: chromatin (coiled DNA chromosomes) · nucleolus (genes encoding rRNA) · nuclear envelope (double membrane) · nuclear pores (passageways in and out).

4. Transcription vs translation — the language rule

  • Transcribe = copy text within the same language. DNA nucleotides → mRNA nucleotides (still G, C, A, T/U). Happens in the nucleus.
  • Translate = copy text into a new language. mRNA nucleotides → amino acids. Happens at the rough ER: tRNA reads the mRNA, fetches the amino acid, the ribosome assembles the chain.

Clinical note — Hurler's syndrome

Lysosomal storage diseases: inherited enzyme deficiencies leave material undegraded, so it builds up in tissues. In Hurler's syndrome, mucopolysaccharides accumulate in many tissues including the cornea. Freddo's line: you could be the one who makes the diagnosis.
