Blepharitis

Inflammation of the lid margin: the staphylococcal form in full (signs, corneal complications, treatment step by step), then seborrheic, mixed, meibomian seborrheic and angular blepharitis.

Review: pus is "dead white blood cells"; madarosis = lash loss ("I'm mad I lost my lashes"); trichiasis = inward-turning lashes ("tricky eyelashes"); lids "thickened, hardened, red"; telangiectatic vessels. Key facts

Staphylococcus = main cause · pus = dead PMNs + protein = bacterial infection
Warm compresses 10–15 min bid–qid → lid scrubs → ± antibiotic ointment (bacitracin, erythromycin, gentamicin, tobramycin) → ± doxycycline 100 mg PO bid × 7–10 d
PEE: inferior third (staph) vs interpalpebral (seborrheic); stains
Marginal infiltrate: clear zone to the limbus, does not stain, steroid or steroid–antibiotic (Tobradex, Zylet)
Phlyctenule: type IV, was TB, now staph again
Angular: Moraxella (wet) → zinc sulfate 0.25% · staph (dry) → bacitracin or erythromycin
Chronic: control, not cure

Self-check

Anterior vs posterior blepharitis: what is each centred on, and which forms belong to each?
Anterior: the lashes and follicles (staphylococcal, seborrheic, mixed). Posterior: the meibomian glands and orifices (meibomian seborrheic, meibomian gland dysfunction). Lecture 2 · p. 3
List the signs of bacterial lid infection the exam guidelines name.
Pus discharge; lashes with crusts/flakes, madarosis, trichiasis; lids thickened, notched, red; telangiectatic blood vessels. Guidelines · p. 1 · Audio · 07:07–07:52
How does staph cause inflammation besides infecting the follicles?
It makes lipase, which breaks down the tear lipids into free fatty acids that trigger an inflammatory and irritative response. Lecture 2 · p. 5
What are crusts, and what is a collarette in staph blepharitis?
Crusts are dried pus along the lash line at the lash bases; a crust that encircles the lash is a collarette. Lecture 2 · p. 8, 11
Give the two steps of lid hygiene with timing.
Warm wet compress on the closed lids 10–15 min bid–qid, then lid scrubs bid–qid (commercial cleanser or hypochlorous acid 0.02%; baby shampoo no longer recommended because it dries). Lecture 2 · p. 23–26
When is oral doxycycline used, at what dose, and why does it work even on resistant bacteria?
When unusually severe: 100 mg PO bid × 7–10 days. It inhibits bacterial lipase synthesis and staph exotoxin-induced cytokines and chemokines, an effect independent of killing the bacteria. Lecture 2 · p. 29
Marginal infiltrate vs PEE: where, does it stain, why?
PEE: divots in the epithelium, inferior third, stains with fluorescein, from exotoxins. Infiltrate: subepithelial white cells in the periphery with a clear zone to the limbus, does not stain (unless the epithelium above is damaged), a hypersensitivity (Ag–Ab–complement) reaction. Lecture 2 · p. 20–21, 33–34
How is a marginal infiltrate or a phlyctenule treated?
Treat the blepharitis and add a topical steroid (prednisolone acetate, dexamethasone, loteprednol) q2h–qid, or a steroid–antibiotic combination (Tobradex, Zylet). Lecture 2 · p. 37, 40 · Audio · 08:57–09:26
What used to be a primary cause of phlyctenule, and what is now?
Tuberculosis (immigrants from endemic areas, or locally acquired active TB); now staphylococcus again. Lecture 2 · p. 41
Seborrheic blepharitis: what do you see, and where is the PEE?
Greasy, waxy, easily flaking scales at the lash bases (scurf), slightly greasy lid skin; interpalpebral PEE. Often with seborrheic dermatitis (dandruff). Lecture 2 · p. 42–46
Meibomian seborrheic blepharitis: signs and treatment?
Frothy tears, oil slick in the tear film, reduced TBUT, conjunctival injection, normal lid tissue. Keep the margins clean, lubricants. Lecture 2 · p. 49–50
Angular blepharitis: two forms and their treatment?
Staphylococcal (dry, scaly): bacitracin or erythromycin. Moraxella (wet, macerated, whitish frothy discharge): zinc sulfate 0.25%. All forms get antibiotic ointment. Lecture 2 · p. 51–53
