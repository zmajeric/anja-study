Chapter 8 · Lecture 8 "Lacrimal System Diseases"

Lacrimal diseases

The tap, the sink and the sewer: what goes wrong in the tear drainage of infants and adults, in the lacrimal sac (dacryocystitis), in the lacrimal gland (dacryoadenitis) and in the canaliculus, and how each is treated.

What the exam asks

From the exam guidelines and the professor's review, the points that touch this lecture:

  • Exam "Dacryoadenitis signs / causes" Guidelines · p. 2 → §6. The professor: dacryoadenitis is inflammation of the lacrimal gland (the "tap", which makes water); it gives an S-shaped upper lid, so look at pictures of it. Clinical correlate · nasolacrimal massage

The parent places an index finger over the infant's canaliculi (medial corner of the eyelid) and makes several slow downward strokes. The motion sends hydrostatic forces down the nasolacrimal duct to open the obstruction. Lecture 8 · p. 6

Do not miss: congenital glaucoma

Congenital glaucoma is in the differential of congenital tearing. If the child has both tearing and photophobia, consider congenital glaucoma even if the cornea is not cloudy or large. Lecture 8 · p. 9 Your note on the next slide: "unusually large eyes can be a sign of glaucoma." Your notes

Chronic dacryocystitis and carcinoma of the sac

Chronic dacryocystitis = an enlarged lacrimal sac without signs of acute inflammation. Etiology: chronic infection · chronic inflammation / granuloma · tumour (cancer). Management: culture to choose the antibiotic, then DCR; if not infectious, biopsy and appropriate treatment. Lecture 8 · p. 24 The next slide shows a carcinoma of the lacrimal sac (photo only). Self-check

A 3-week-old has had a watery, crusty eye since birth; pressing over the lacrimal sac pushes mucus out of the punctum. Diagnosis and first-line management?
Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction (failure of the duct to canalize, usually at the valve of Hasner). Warm compresses and massage bid–qid (slow downward strokes over the canaliculi), erythromycin ointment if mucopurulent; > 90 % resolve; probe at 6–13 months if not. Lecture 8 · p. 4–7
A tearing infant also has photophobia but the corneas look normal. What must you rule out?
Congenital glaucoma: tearing plus photophobia means consider it even if the cornea is not cloudy or large. Lecture 8 · p. 9
What is a congenital dacryocele, what does it look like, and how is it treated?
Amniotic fluid trapped in an obstructed lacrimal sac: a firm bluish mass on the side of the nose below the medial canthus. Hot compresses and massage first, but most infants need probing. Lecture 8 · p. 3
Give the signs of acute dacryocystitis and its complications.
Tenderness, erythema and swelling over the nasal lower lid; epiphora; purulent punctal discharge on pressure over the sac; maybe fever and malaise. Complications: preseptal cellulitis, conjunctivitis, cutaneous fistulas, less often orbital cellulitis and sepsis. Lecture 8 · p. 16
How is acute dacryocystitis treated, and what is done once it has settled?
Warm compresses tid, systemic antibiotic (oral Augmentin or IV Unasyn), erythromycin ointment if conjunctivitis, aspirate the sac (19 G) for culture; incision and drainage if a pointing abscess. Afterwards: dilation/irrigation or probing, nasal exam, maybe CT, and consider DCR with silicone tubing. Lecture 8 · p. 22–23
What is a dacryocystorhinostomy?
An anastomosis between the lacrimal sac and the nasal cavity, the surgical fix for a nasolacrimal duct obstruction that dilation, irrigation or probing could not open (often with silicone tubing). Lecture 8 · p. 12, 23
Dacryoadenitis: where, what sign, which lobe, and how do you see it?
Lacrimal gland, temporal upper lid; S-shaped lid deformity; the palpebral lobe is affected more often, seen in the supratemporal fornix by lifting the upper lid while the patient looks down. Proptosis if the orbital lobe is involved. Lecture 8 · p. 27–30
Name the infectious causes of acute dacryoadenitis and the treatment for each.
Mumps / EBV: warm compresses. HSV / HZV: oral acyclovir or famciclovir. Staph / strep: Augmentin. N. gonorrhoeae: ceftriaxone. Mycobacteria: excision plus isoniazid and rifampin. Treponema pallidum: penicillin G. Lecture 8 · p. 27, 31
Chronic dacryoadenitis: how common, signs, causes?
More common than acute. Enlarged gland, globe displacement, restricted motility. Causes: sarcoidosis, thyroid ophthalmopathy, Sjögren's syndrome, tumours. Lecture 8 · p. 32
What is the Sjögren triad, and what happens to the lacrimal gland?
Dry eye, dry mouth, arthritis; autoimmune. The gland is infiltrated by lymphocytes and may enlarge. Lecture 8 · p. 35
A preschool child has a mobile, nontender superotemporal mass, well circumscribed on CT. Diagnosis and treatment?
Dermoid cyst, the look-alike of chronic dacryoadenitis; surgical excision. Lecture 8 · p. 38
Canaliculitis: the organism with sulfur granules, the signs and the management.
Actinomyces israelii (filamentous gram + rod) forms sulfur granules. Red swollen punctum, medial conjunctivitis, discharge expressed from the punctum. Warm compresses, culture, remove concretions (express or scrape), systemic and topical antibiotics. Lecture 8 · p. 39–43
