Allergic lid disease

Five itchy (or not so itchy) skin reactions around the eye: irritant and allergic contact dermatitis, atopic dermatitis, urticaria and angioedema. Know which one itches, which one gets a steroid, and which eye drops cause it.

2 · Contact dermatitis: definitions

A very common immunological disease from direct contact with an environmental allergen. Dermatitis = any inflammation of the epidermis. Contact dermatitis = any skin inflammation when the skin surface contacts a substance from outside the body. Episodes can be triggered by specific events. Lecture 7 · p. 8

Differential and treatment

DDx: make sure the edema is not caused by renal or cardiac disease; contact dermatitis. Treatment: find the cause and avoid it; acute phase: cold compresses · antihistamine · systemic steroids (prednisone, "unpredictable") · epinephrine s.c. for acute pharyngeal or laryngeal angioedema. Key facts

Type I: IgE, mast cells, histamine, minutes–hours · Type IV: T-cells, hours–days
ICD: 1–24 h, patch test negative, no steroids · ACD: 48 h, patch 48 h on, read 72 h–1 wk, hydrocortisone <1% BID–TID 5–10 d, cold compresses 4–6×/day
Neomycin: ~20% of users, after ≥5 days (audio) · AD: hydrocortisone 1%, cool compress 15–30 min, subsides by age 3–4
Urticaria: 10–25% of people, wheals 1–5 mm to >20 mm, cause found in 70%

Self-check

What is the main allergy symptom, and which of the lecture's reactions may lack it?
Itch. Irritant contact dermatitis may have no itching (pain, burning, stinging instead); angioedema has little pruritus. Audio · 12:37 · Lecture 7 · p. 10, 56
Type I vs type IV reaction: cells, mediators, timing.
Type I: IgE-sensitized mast cells and basophils release histamine and ECF; minutes to hours. Type IV: sensitized T-cells, cytotoxicity or lymphokines, cell-mediated; hours to days. Lecture 7 · p. 3–4
What is a Dennie–Morgan fold?
A fold or line in the skin below the lower lid caused by edema in allergic dermatitis: a key allergic sign. Lecture 7 · p. 5
Which contact dermatitis is NOT treated with steroids, and how is it managed?
Irritant contact dermatitis. Avoid the irritant (barriers), non-sensitizing moisturizer (petroleum jelly), mild cleansers and low-irritant soaps, alcohol cleansers with moisturizer; no topical steroids. Lecture 7 · p. 14 · Audio · 13:03
Name the topical ocular drugs on the slide that cause ACD, and the biggest culprit.
Antazoline, neomycin, phenylephrine, atropine, pilocarpine, anti-glaucoma drugs. Neomycin is the biggest culprit (~20% of users, after about 5 days). Lecture 7 · p. 21 · Audio · 13:38
How is ACD diagnosed with a patch test?
Suspected materials on patches, left 48 hours, read again between 72 hours and 1 week; positive = erythema, papules, edema, vesicles, correlated with the history, location and course. Lecture 7 · p. 30
Give the full ACD treatment list.
Remove the allergen; cold compresses 4–6×/day; topical steroid BID–TID 5–10 days (<1% hydrocortisone); oral antihistamine (diphenhydramine); lubricants 4–6×/day; topical immunomodulator BID (pimecrolimus, tacrolimus). Lecture 7 · p. 33
Atopic dermatitis: where in infants, where in adults, and which infections threaten?
Infants: face (cheeks), scalp, knees, exudative/vesicular. Adults: elbows, neck, back of knees, ankles, dry and lichenified. Secondary Staph/Strep infection; vaccinia, herpes simplex, molluscum contagiosum. Lecture 7 · p. 37, 40, 45
Urticaria vs angioedema: layer, look, itch, danger.
Urticaria: superficial dermis, raised itchy wheals with flare. Angioedema: deep dermis, skin looks normal, extensive swelling of face, lips, lids, tongue, throat; little itch, maybe pain or burning; may cause respiratory distress. Lecture 7 · p. 53, 56
Treat acute angioedema of the throat. What must you rule out first?
Rule out edema from renal or cardiac disease (and contact dermatitis). Treat: find and avoid the cause, cold compresses, antihistamine, systemic steroids, and subcutaneous epinephrine for pharyngeal or laryngeal angioedema. Lecture 7 · p. 61
