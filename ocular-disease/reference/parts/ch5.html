Draining a pointed hordeolum

find the pointing site→stab incision: 18-gauge needle or #11 blade→several stabs if several pockets→leave open, clean margin
  • External incisions scar: avoid them unless the hordeolum already points externally.
  • Internal incisions run vertically, to minimize the area of cornea swept by a scar during blinking.

Key facts

Hordeolum: S. aureus 90–95% · external = Zeis, skin side · internal = meibomian, conjunctival side
Warm soaks qid × 15 min · drain with 18-gauge needle / #11 blade · internal incision vertical · systemic antibiotics only if spreading
Chalazion: compresses 10–15 min, qid (q2h better) → triamcinolone 0.1–0.2 ml through conjunctiva → I&C (clamp, conjunctival side, ointment × 2 days)
Moll = sweat, translucent, recurs after aspiration → excise sac · Zeis = oil, less translucent
Sebaceous cyst: punctum, cheesy white · remove total sac · oral antibiotic if infected
Lipid = meibomian · aqueous = lacrimal · mucin = Krause, Wolfring, goblet cells

Self-check

Which gland is affected in an internal hordeolum, and which in an external one?
Internal: meibomian gland (points to the conjunctiva). External: gland of Zeis (points to the skin, near the lashes). Lecture 5 · p. 2, 5, 10
What organism, what lesion type, and which history and risk factors?
S. aureus in 90–95%; an abscess. Painful localized swelling of one lid, often recurrent, no constitutional signs. Diabetes, debilitating illness, chronic blepharitis, seborrhea, high serum lipids. Lecture 5 · p. 2–4
A hordeolum: when do you add topical antibiotics, and when systemic ones?
Topical: recurrent or actively draining lesions. Systemic: only when inflammation spreads beyond the immediate area (e.g. preseptal cellulitis). Lecture 5 · p. 9, 14–15
How do you drain a pointed internal hordeolum, and why that way?
Stab at the pointing site with an 18-gauge needle or #11 blade, from inside, vertically (least cornea swept by the scar); avoid skin incisions (scar) unless it already points out; several stabs for several pockets; leave open with a clean margin. Lecture 5 · p. 16
Define a chalazion and say how it feels.
Meibomian cyst: lipogranulomatous infiltration of a meibomian gland, may follow an internal hordeolum; firm nodule within the tarsal plate, non-tender, non-inflamed. Lecture 5 · p. 17
List the chalazion treatment ladder with the details of each step.
Warm compresses 10–15 min, qid (q2h better), best when recent → triamcinolone 0.1–0.2 ml through the conjunctiva → incision and curettage: local anesthetic, clamp, conjunctival incision, scrape, antibiotic ointment × 2 days. Lecture 5 · p. 24
Cyst of Moll vs cyst of Zeis: gland, content, look, treatment.
Moll: sweat gland of the lashes (sudoriferous cyst, apocrine hidrocystoma), translucent fluid-filled dome, non-tender; cosmetic; aspiration recurs → excise with the sac. Zeis: oil gland of the lashes, oily, less translucent; treat like Moll (your note). Lecture 5 · p. 26–31
How do you recognize and treat a sebaceous cyst, and how is it different from xanthelasma?
Yellowish-white lump with a central punctum, cheesy white content; remove the total sac (squeezing → recurrence), oral antibiotic if infected. Xanthelasma: flat yellow fatty plaques, both lids, symmetrical. Lecture 5 · p. 33–37
Name each gland and its secretion: meibomian, Zeis, Moll, sebaceous, lacrimal, Krause/Wolfring/goblet.
Meibomian: lipid. Zeis: oil (lashes). Moll: sweat (slide) / oils for the lashes (audio). Sebaceous: cheesy white material. Lacrimal: aqueous. Krause, Wolfring, goblet cells: mucin. Zeis, Moll, meibomian are modified sebaceous glands. Guidelines · p. 2 · Audio · 15:07–16:42
