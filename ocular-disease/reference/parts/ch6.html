Chapter 6 · Lecture 6 "Preseptal and Orbital Cellulitis"

Preseptal & orbital cellulitis (+ poliosis, vitiligo)

Two lid infections that look alike from the front: one sits in front of the orbital septum and is treated with pills at home, the other sits behind it and can blind or kill. The lecture closes with two pigment disorders of lids and lashes.

1 · The one structure that decides it: the orbital septum

The orbital septum divides the two diseases. Preseptal cellulitis involves the periorbital tissue anterior to the septum; orbital cellulitis is infection of orbital soft tissue posterior to the septum, involving fat and muscles. Self-check

Which anatomical structure separates preseptal from orbital cellulitis, and on which side is each?
The orbital septum. Preseptal = anterior to it (lid and periorbital tissue, globe not affected); orbital = posterior to it (orbital fat and muscles). Lecture 6 · p. 4, 36
A swollen red lid: which three signs does Prof. Yudcovitch use to call it orbital?
Pupils (APD), motilities (restricted, painful EOMs) and proptosis, with fever. Their absence points to preseptal. Audio · 16:42–17:20 · Lecture 6 · p. 46, 59
Name the three main sources of preseptal cellulitis with examples.
(1) Direct inoculation: laceration, strabismus surgery, insect bite. (2) Spread from nearby local infection: URI, sinusitis, dacryocystitis, acute hordeolum. (3) Hematogenous spread: otitis media, pneumonia. URI is the usual cause when no trauma or lid infection is found. Lecture 6 · p. 6–10
First-line oral treatment of preseptal cellulitis in an adult, and the alternatives if allergic to penicillin or MRSA is a concern.
Amoxicillin/clavulanate (Augmentin) 500/125 mg TID × 10 days (mild–moderate) or 875/125 mg BID × 10–14 days (severe), with follow-up. Penicillin allergy or MRSA: trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Bactrim DS) or doxycycline 100 mg BID (not in children, pregnancy, nursing). Clindamycin and ceftriaxone are other options. Lecture 6 · p. 27–31
When is a patient with preseptal cellulitis hospitalised, and with which IV drugs?
Moderate–severe disease, toxic patient, noncompliant patient, child under 5, no improvement or worsening after a few days. IV ampicillin-sulbactam (Unasyn), ceftriaxone, or vancomycin if MRSA is suspected. Lecture 6 · p. 32–33
When is a CT recommended in preseptal cellulitis?
Lids cannot be separated; orbital involvement cannot be ruled out clinically; fever and leukocytosis; progression despite antibiotics; young children in whom a reliable exam is difficult. Lecture 6 · p. 24–25
What is the most common cause of orbital cellulitis, and which question must you always ask?
Bacterial sinusitis, mostly ethmoid sinusitis. Ask about recent dental work (teeth are a source). Lecture 6 · p. 39–40
Treat orbital cellulitis: setting, drugs, duration, and when to operate.
Hospitalise every patient. IV broad-spectrum antibiotics STAT (ampicillin-sulbactam a good first choice) for 1–2 weeks, then oral for 2–3 weeks; vancomycin etc. for MRSA; amphotericin B for fungal (Mucor, Aspergillus). Imaging (CT with contrast) is essential. Drain surgically if no response in 2–3 days, VA drops, an APD develops, proptosis progresses, the abscess does not shrink; craniotomy for a non-responding brain abscess. Lecture 6 · p. 50–55
List the five CT stages of orbital involvement.
I preseptal cellulitis · II inflammatory orbital edema · III subperiosteal abscess · IV orbital abscess · V cavernous sinus thrombosis. Lecture 6 · p. 51
What is poliosis, and which three genetic syndromes cause it?
A localized patch of white hair from decreased or absent melanin in the bulb or follicle. Genetic: piebaldism, Waardenburg (with sensorineural deafness), tuberous sclerosis. It may be the first sign of melanoma. Lecture 6 · p. 61–65
Describe vitiligo lesions and name three treatments.
White, depigmented, well-demarcated macules and patches, round/oval/linear, enlarging outward; usually bilateral on the lids, periocular/perioral; asymptomatic; onset before 20; linked to thyroid abnormalities. Treatment: sun protection, camouflage, topical steroids, PUVA, narrow-band UVB, grafting, depigmentation. Lecture 6 · p. 69–74
