Which nerve closes the lid, and which disease shows its loss?
The facial nerve (CN VII), through the orbicularis. Bell's palsy: the lid does not close, the forehead does not wrinkle, the nasolabial fold is flat, all on one side. Lecture 1 · p. 6–8 · Audio · 06:24

Levator: striated, CN III. Müller's muscle: smooth, sympathetic, from the underside of the levator into the superior tarsus. Lecture 1 · p. 10

Palpebral portion: blinking, voluntary winking, keeps the lid tight to the globe. Orbital portion: forced closure. Lecture 1 · p. 4

Down and out; diabetes. Audio · 06:46–06:53

Sebum (lipid, oil): the oily (top) layer of the tear film. About 25 per tarsus, opening just behind the lashes. Lecture 1 · p. 13, 17 · Audio · 11:35
Which gland is the only one with reflex tearing, and what does it make?
The lacrimal gland; aqueous (water). Lecture 1 · p. 16 · Audio · 15:46

Self-check

Anterior vs posterior blepharitis: what is each centred on, and which forms belong to each?
Anterior: the lashes and follicles (staphylococcal, seborrheic, mixed). Posterior: the meibomian glands and orifices (meibomian seborrheic, meibomian gland dysfunction). Lecture 2 · p. 3
List the signs of bacterial lid infection the exam guidelines name.
Pus discharge; lashes with crusts/flakes, madarosis, trichiasis; lids thickened, notched, red; telangiectatic blood vessels. Guidelines · p. 1 · Audio · 07:07–07:52
How does staph cause inflammation besides infecting the follicles?
It makes lipase, which breaks down the tear lipids into free fatty acids that trigger an inflammatory and irritative response. Lecture 2 · p. 5
What are crusts, and what is a collarette in staph blepharitis?
Crusts are dried pus along the lash line at the lash bases; a crust that encircles the lash is a collarette. Lecture 2 · p. 8, 11
Give the two steps of lid hygiene with timing.
Warm wet compress on the closed lids 10–15 min bid–qid, then lid scrubs bid–qid (commercial cleanser or hypochlorous acid 0.02%; baby shampoo no longer recommended because it dries). Lecture 2 · p. 23–26
When is oral doxycycline used, at what dose, and why does it work even on resistant bacteria?
When unusually severe: 100 mg PO bid × 7–10 days. It inhibits bacterial lipase synthesis and staph exotoxin-induced cytokines and chemokines, an effect independent of killing the bacteria. Lecture 2 · p. 29
Marginal infiltrate vs PEE: where, does it stain, why?
PEE: divots in the epithelium, inferior third, stains with fluorescein, from exotoxins. Infiltrate: subepithelial white cells in the periphery with a clear zone to the limbus, does not stain (unless the epithelium above is damaged), a hypersensitivity (Ag–Ab–complement) reaction. Lecture 2 · p. 20–21, 33–34
How is a marginal infiltrate or a phlyctenule treated?
Treat the blepharitis and add a topical steroid (prednisolone acetate, dexamethasone, loteprednol) q2h–qid, or a steroid–antibiotic combination (Tobradex, Zylet). Lecture 2 · p. 37, 40 · Audio · 08:57–09:26
What used to be a primary cause of phlyctenule, and what is now?
Tuberculosis (immigrants from endemic areas, or locally acquired active TB); now staphylococcus again. Lecture 2 · p. 41
Seborrheic blepharitis: what do you see, and where is the PEE?
Greasy, waxy, easily flaking scales at the lash bases (scurf), slightly greasy lid skin; interpalpebral PEE. Often with seborrheic dermatitis (dandruff). Lecture 2 · p. 42–46
Meibomian seborrheic blepharitis: signs and treatment?
Frothy tears, oil slick in the tear film, reduced TBUT, conjunctival injection, normal lid tissue. Keep the margins clean, lubricants. Lecture 2 · p. 49–50
Angular blepharitis: two forms and their treatment?
Staphylococcal (dry, scaly): bacitracin or erythromycin. Moraxella (wet, macerated, whitish frothy discharge): zinc sulfate 0.25%. All forms get antibiotic ointment. Lecture 2 · p. 51–53

A 3-week-old has had a watery, crusty eye since birth; pressing over the lacrimal sac pushes mucus out of the punctum. Diagnosis and first-line management?

Give the signs of acute dacryocystitis and its complications.

Molluscum details. Lesion crush/squash preparations confirm Henderson-Paterson bodies (intracytoplasmic virions); viral replication causes epithelial proliferation with the umbilicated core Lecture 12 · p. 137. Topical treatments: podophyllotoxin 0.5% (fetus toxicity, avoid in pregnancy), iodine, salicylic acid, potassium hydroxide, tretinoin, cantharidin (blistering agent), imiquimod (T-cell modifier; not recommended for children: possible adverse events and not effective in them); oral: cimetidine Lecture 12 · p. 140. Key facts

1 cm: macule/patch · papule/plaque · nodule/tumor · vesicle/bulla · petechia/purpura
BCC = most common malignant lid tumour · SCC = 2nd most common skin cancer, 3rd most common ocular tumour
Melanoma: ~3% of primary skin cancers, 79% of skin-cancer deaths
Clark I = in situ, 100% cure · Clark IV = ~50% dead in 10 yr · Breslow 0.75 mm → 0% metastases · 0.85 mm → 97% 10-yr survival · 3.6 mm → 50% dead in 10 yr
ELM: ×10, oil medium, polarized light in new versions
Actinic keratosis 2–5 mm · seborrheic keratosis 1–6 mm · lichen planus papules 2–4 mm, ages 30–60, ~1% of new derm patients
Keratoacanthoma up to 2 cm · lentigo maligna: age >40, mean 65, 15% malignancy risk · dysplastic nevus < 8–10 mm
Acrochordon incidence 46% · molluscum type I >95%, type II in 60% of HIV infections, 100+ lesions → consider HIV, ~1/3 recur · podophyllotoxin 0.5%

Self-check

Name the lesion: flat discoloration 1.5 cm; raised solid superficial 5 mm; serous-fluid elevation 2 cm; blood deposit 3 mm.
Patch · papule · bulla · petechia. The cut-off is always 1 cm. Lecture 12 · p. 4
Define sessile, pedunculated, umbilicated and papillomatous, with one lesion for each.
Sessile: fixed on a broad base (sessile papilloma). Pedunculated: on a stalk (papilloma, acrochordon). Umbilicated: central crater like a belly button (BCC, molluscum). Papillomatous: cauliflower/artichoke surface (papilloma). Lecture 12 · p. 5, 103, 108, 61, 136 · Audio · 26:01–26:29
Give five differences between a benign and a malignant tumour.
Malignant: metastasises to lymph nodes and distant organs; may kill wherever it occurs if untreated; infiltrates surrounding tissue; may recur after excision; usually grows faster. Benign: kills only in a vital organ; grows by expansion, usually within a capsule; usually does not recur. Lecture 12 · p. 9
What are the four ways to treat a tumour, and the ABCDE rule?
Chemotherapy, cryotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery. Asymmetry, Borders, Color, Diameter, (Elevation) Evolving. Lecture 12 · p. 11, 14
Ephelis, solar lentigo, simple lentigo: what separates them?
Ephelis: larger melanocytes, normal number, autosomal dominant, intensified by sun. Solar lentigo: normal number of melanocytes, appears with sunlight and persists without it, middle-aged to older, treated by laser. Simple lentigo: any age, no link to sun or skin type, does not darken in sunlight. Lecture 12 · p. 16, 20, 23–24
Which nevus type is most common, which may convert to melanoma, and what does a dysplastic nevus look like?
Dermal is most common; junctional (at the dermoepidermal junction, flat) may convert to melanoma. Dysplastic: "fried egg", pigmented or amelanotic, often at the lid margin, < 8–10 mm; melanoma risk if multiple with family history. Lecture 12 · p. 26–27
Superficial spreading vs nodular melanoma.
Superficial spreading: rapid increase in size, lower incidence of metastases. Nodular: vertical growth, deeper penetration, metastasises early. Both: refer for biopsy. Lecture 12 · p. 40, 43
Give Clark's levels I–V and the Breslow depths with their outcomes. Which is more accurate?
Clark I epidermis (in situ, 100% cure) · II invades papillary dermis · III fills papillary dermis · IV invades reticular dermis (~50% dead in 10 yr) · V deep subcutaneous tissue. Breslow 0.75 mm → 0% metastases · 0.85 mm → 97% 10-yr survival · 3.6 mm → 50% dead in 10 yr. Breslow is more accurate. Lecture 12 · p. 41
What is epiluminescence microscopy, and what are its other names?
A hand-held ×10 skin microscope with oil between instrument and skin (new versions: polarized light) that removes surface reflections and shows subsurface structures in vivo; for small equivocal pigmented lesions. Also dermatoscopy, dermoscopy, skin surface microscopy, incidence light microscopy. Lecture 12 · p. 51–52
Describe a basal cell carcinoma, and list its treatments.
Most common malignant lid tumour; UV, elderly, fair skin. Sclerosing or noduloulcerative; early a vascularized nodule; pearly rolled borders, central umbilicated ulceration; skin-coloured. Metastasis uncommon; inner canthal lesions need faster attention. Treatment: AB-steroid if infected, derm consult, excision and repair, radiation, cryo, curettage, electrodessication, imiquimod, 5-FU, PDT, education. A "recurrent chalazion" in an older patient that keeps coming back: what must you think of?
Meibomian gland carcinoma, a sebaceous gland carcinoma that is mistaken for a persistent, recurrent chalazion and can mimic chronic lid/conjunctival infection; excision, radiation. Lecture 12 · p. 82
Actinic keratosis vs keratoacanthoma vs seborrheic keratosis: cause and look.
Actinic: UV, squamous cell dysplasia, dry scaly 2–5 mm, premalignant. Keratoacanthoma: from sebaceous glands, benign squamous proliferation, fast-growing nodule up to 2 cm with a keratin-filled crater, regresses; R/O SCC. Seborrheic: basal cell hyperplasia, waxy/greasy 1–6 mm plaques, "barnacles of old age", most common benign epidermal tumour in the elderly. Lecture 12 · p. 87, 92–93, 112–113
Which lid lesions are caused by viruses, and by which virus?
Verrucae: HPV. Molluscum contagiosum: poxvirus (DNA). Herpetic lesions: herpes simplex virus and varicella zoster virus (shingles, Hutchinson's sign). Lecture 12 · p. 124, 135, 147–149 · Audio · 26:48–26:59
A child has many pearly umbilicated papules on the lids and a follicular conjunctivitis. Diagnosis, confirmation, management?
Molluscum contagiosum. Crush prep shows Henderson-Paterson bodies. If quiet leave alone; express if central discharge; excision, cryo, topical acids; not imiquimod in children; teach hygiene; 100+ lesions → consider HIV; rule out BCC. Lecture 12 · p. 136–140
